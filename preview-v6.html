Reviewing AI-Generated Labs: What I Catch and Why

The challenge
AI-generated labs read fluently but hide accuracy gaps: solutions that do more than the task asks, validation that does not actually pass, instructions that drift from the lab outline, or describe tools the lab environment does not have.
My contribution
Review AI-generated software-development labs against a structured rubric: validation and solution hygiene first, then alignment, relevance, and style, with feedback written to be constructive and clear enough to improve the AI prompting itself.
The result
On one C# debugging lab: 7 issues flagged across 5 steps in the first round (4 required fixes, 3 suggested improvements), caught in review before learners ever saw them. After a second round of review and code testing, all of the suggested and required changes were implemented, and the lab is now published.
Audience
Pluralsight learners, who get labs that work, and lab authors, who get feedback they can act on in one pass.

How I review: blockers first, content second

  1. Run validation. Do all tasks and validations pass? Does the solution code contain only what the task requests?
  2. Review content once major blockers are resolved, against the rubric below.

In practice: a C# debugging lab

The lab: Fixing a C# Application with C# Failure Stack Traces. Learners reproduce crashes, read stack traces, and fix bugs across five steps.

The crash that couldn't crash Required fix

What the AI generated

Task 2.1 asks learners to reproduce a crash, but the starter parser does not throw an exception. There is nothing to investigate.

What I flagged

A debugging lab where the bug does not exist. The task is uncompletable as written: a blocker, not a style nit.

My feedback: "Provide a mostly working parser with an intentional bug so learners can investigate the exception and stack trace before fixing it."

Why it matters: Learners cannot practice debugging if there is nothing to debug.
Error taxonomy: how I grade what I find

Not every flag is equal. Required means the lab does not work, teaches the wrong thing, or misleads a learner. Suggested means the lab works, but a change would help learners focus or trust the steps.

How I work with AI

  • I use AI output as a first draft to review, not as a finished lab to publish.
  • I never let AI output go unchecked on validation, code, or safety steps.
  • I write feedback so the next draft improves, not just this draft.
  • I do not claim prompt engineering expertise beyond that structured feedback.
